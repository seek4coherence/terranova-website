How to Stop Foreclosure in Kentucky

Foreclosure is a process with dates, not a single event. That matters, because every stage has a different set of options. This guide explains how the Kentucky process works, what actually stops a sale, and where the equity usually goes.

Read the Court Documents First

If you have been served with a foreclosure complaint, the most important information in your life is printed on those pages: the case number, the court, the response deadline, and the amount claimed. Nothing in a general guide overrides what is in your case file.

Kentucky is a judicial foreclosure state. The lender files a lawsuit in circuit court, you are served, and you have a limited window to respond. If the case proceeds, the judge can enter a judgment and order the property sold, typically by the master commissioner. That sequence takes months, which is why acting early matters so much.

If you cannot afford an attorney, contact Legal Aid or a local legal services office. A response filed on time can preserve defenses that are lost forever if you do nothing.

The Options That Actually Work

  1. Reinstate the loan. Most mortgages allow you to cure the default by paying the past-due amount plus fees before a deadline. This is usually the cheapest option if you can find the money.
  2. Loan modification or forbearance. Ask the lender's loss mitigation department in writing. Federal and investor guidelines change, so ask what programs currently apply to your loan rather than assuming.
  3. Sell the property before the sale date. If there is equity, a sale converts it into cash instead of losing it at auction. This can be a retail listing if you have time, or a cash sale if you do not.
  4. Chapter 13 bankruptcy. Filing generally pauses the sale and can let you repay arrears over a plan. It is a real option, but it is a serious legal step that requires a bankruptcy attorney.
  5. Deed in lieu or short sale. If the house is worth less than the debt, the lender may agree to take the property or approve a sale for less than owed. Get any deficiency waiver in writing.

What Usually Does Not Work

Be skeptical of anyone who promises to "stop the auction" for an upfront fee, or who asks you to sign a deed now and keep paying them rent with a promise to buy it back later. Those arrangements have a name in the industry, and they frequently end with the family losing the house and the equity.

Also be skeptical of waiting. The most common mistake is doing nothing because the sale date feels far away. Title work, payoff statements, and lender response times all take weeks, and a buyer cannot close in three days just because the calendar got short.

Where the Equity Goes

If you owe $80,000 on a house worth $140,000, there is $60,000 of equity at stake, minus costs. In a private sale before the auction, that difference is yours after the loan is paid. At a foreclosure auction, the property is sold to the highest bidder, and any surplus is distributed according to the court's priority order, which can mean delays and claims from other lienholders.

That is the practical argument for selling early. It is not about avoiding the foreclosure out of pride; it is about not donating your equity to the auction process.
