Selling a House With Tenants

A tenant does not have to be an obstacle to a sale, but it does change who will buy the property and what they will pay. This guide covers how leases survive a sale, what happens to deposits, and the real math on selling occupied versus waiting for vacancy.

The Lease Survives the Sale

This is the fact that surprises most sellers. Selling a rental property does not cancel an existing lease. The new owner steps into the landlord's shoes and takes the property subject to the tenant's rights under that lease. If the tenant has eight months left, the new owner has a tenant for eight months.

That is not a legal technicality; it is the core reason occupied rentals trade at a different price and to a different buyer. An owner-occupant cannot move in until the lease ends, so they usually will not compete. An investor who wants income will, and investors price for the work and the risk.

Who Actually Buys an Occupied Rental

  • Buy-and-hold investors. They want the income stream and will pay for a paying tenant with a clean lease.
  • Direct cash buyers. They can close without a lender, which matters because many lenders will not finance an owner-occupant loan on a tenant-occupied property.
  • Section 8 and workforce housing operators. They often prefer occupied units with housing authority paperwork already in place.

What you will not usually get is a retail price from a family that wants to live there. That is the trade: certainty and speed in exchange for the owner-occupant premium.

Security Deposits and Prorations

The security deposit belongs to the tenant. At closing it is normally transferred to the buyer, along with a written statement of the amount held and any prior deductions. Rent is prorated to the closing date. Utility accounts, if the landlord pays any, are transferred or closed.

Put all of this in the purchase agreement. Disputes over deposits are one of the most common post-closing problems in rental sales, and they are entirely avoidable with one paragraph of paperwork.

The Three Ways to Handle the Tenant

  1. Sell occupied. Fastest path. The buyer takes the lease and the tenant stays. Best when the tenant pays, the lease is documented, and the property is in rentable condition.
  2. Wait for the lease to end. Best when the lease expires soon, the tenant is reliable, and the house would show well vacant. You carry the property in the meantime, and the market can move against you.
  3. Negotiate an early exit. A cash-for-keys agreement pays the tenant to vacate voluntarily by a set date. It is often cheaper than an eviction and much faster, but get it in writing and follow Kentucky notice rules.

Why Eviction Is Usually the Wrong First Move

Eviction is a court process with a timeline, a filing cost, and a public record. It can take weeks or months, and during that time the property cannot be shown cleanly or sold to an owner-occupant. If the tenant is not paying, eviction may be necessary. If the tenant is paying but you want them gone, a negotiated exit is almost always cheaper.

Do not change the locks, shut off utilities, or remove belongings to force a tenant out. Those actions create liability and can turn a simple sale into a lawsuit.

The Math on Waiting

Suppose the house would sell for $150,000 vacant and $135,000 occupied. Waiting costs you the difference in price, plus taxes, insurance, maintenance, and the risk that the tenant damages the unit or the market softens. If the gap is $15,000 and waiting takes six months, the carrying cost alone can eat most of it.

Run the numbers honestly before deciding. In many cases the occupied sale wins after costs, even though the headline price is lower.
